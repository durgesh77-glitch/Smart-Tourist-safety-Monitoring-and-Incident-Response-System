Remote routes make rescue a race against time

Most tourist safety gaps aren't caused by carelessness — they're caused by systems that can't see a traveler until something has already gone wrong.

Patchy network coverage

Treks, forest trails and heritage sites often sit outside mobile range, so a distress call reaches help only after a dangerous delay.

Manual ID and permits

Paper permits and photocopied IDs slow down checkposts, and give rescue teams almost nothing to work with when a tourist goes missing.

Fragmented response

Police, park rangers, hotels and hospitals each keep their own records, with no shared view of who is where when an incident starts.

One platform, from check-in to case closed

Digital tourist ID

Issued at the entry point and anchored on a blockchain ledger — tamper-proof, linked to itinerary and emergency contacts, verifiable offline at any checkpost.

Live tracking & geofencing

Opt-in location draws safe-zone boundaries around trails and monuments. Crossing one, or going quiet too long, triggers an automatic check.

One-tap SOS

A wearable band or the app's SOS button sends exact location and last known route to the nearest response unit, no signal search required.

AI anomaly detection

Flags patterns worth a look — a tourist off-route after dark, a check-in missed for hours, or a cluster of alerts from one area.

Unified response console

Police, tourism officers and hospitals work from the same live map and case file, with tasks assigned and tracked in one workflow.

Automated incident record

Every SOS drafts an E-FIR with location, timeline and ID details, ready for an officer to review and confirm — no re-keying at the desk.
